If you're searching for a monitor arm — to raise your monitor to eye level, or to free up depth on your desk — you'll quickly run into a wall of similar-looking products that are hard to tell apart. Monitor arms broadly split into two mount types: clamp-mount (clamps onto the edge of the desk) and grommet-mount (passes through a hole drilled in the desktop) — and which one you use changes how much it affects your desk. Here we compare four products by weight capacity, supported screen size, and mount type.

Pros and Cons of Monitor Arms

Here's a summary of what tends to satisfy people after buying, and what's worth checking before you do.

Pros

  • No monitor stand needed, freeing up desk depth
  • Fine height and angle adjustment makes it easy to match your eye level
  • Cable routing can be bundled and hidden along the arm

Cons

  • Can't be installed if your monitor's weight is close to or over the weight capacity limit
  • If your desktop is too thin or too thick, it may fall outside the clamp's supported range
  • Grommet mounting requires drilling a hole in the desktop

Who Monitor Arms Are a Good Fit For — and Who Should Consider Other Options

As a category, monitor arms tend to work well for the following people.

Good fit if you...

  • Want to free up desk depth by skipping a monitor stand
  • Adjust your monitor's height and angle frequently
  • Want to bundle your cable routing neatly along the arm

Consider other options if you...

  • Have a monitor whose weight is at or well over the arm's weight capacity
  • Are renting and must avoid drilling holes (stick to models supporting clamp mounting)
  • Already have a monitor stand you're happy with and don't need to switch

What to Look at When Choosing

We used the following three criteria for this comparison.

1. Weight capacity (can it handle your monitor's weight?)

2. Supported screen size and VESA standard (does it match the screw-hole pattern on the back of your monitor?)

3. Mount type (does clamp-mount avoid damaging your desk, or does grommet-mount require drilling?)

💡

The VESA standard is the spacing of the screw holes on the back of your monitor — almost always 75×75mm or 100×100mm. Check the manual or the label on the back.

Still deciding? Check these in order

Q1. Does your monitor's weight fall within the weight capacity?

A typical 24–27 inch monitor is usually covered by a model rated around 9kg, but for larger or curved monitors, a model rated 12kg or higher is the safer choice.

›
Q2. Does the VESA standard (screw-hole spacing on the back) match?

If it doesn't match, the arm simply won't mount, so check your monitor's VESA standard before buying.

›
Q3. Can you drill into your desk, or are you renting and need to avoid it?

If you can't drill, stick to clamp-mount models (or ones that support both clamp and grommet mounting).

Common Complaints in Reviews, and How to Handle Them

With gas-spring models like BONTEC and ARES WING, some buyers report the arm gradually sagging over time. Most models include a screw that adjusts spring tension, so re-adjusting it whenever you notice sagging can often fix the problem. If you choose grommet mounting, you'll need to drill a hole in the desktop, so if you're renting and the desk isn't yours — or you'll need to restore it to its original condition — clamp mounting is the safer choice. Some buyers have also found that the VESA standard (screw-hole spacing) on the back of their monitor didn't match a product's supported range, so checking your monitor's VESA standard before buying helps you avoid that problem.

Best Pick by Situation

If you're trying a monitor arm for the first time, BONTEC is the pick. If you're planning to move up to a bigger, heavier monitor, go with ARES WING. If you use a curved monitor or want a solid warranty, FLEXISPOT MA6 is the candidate. And if you want to avoid gas-spring wear and use it for the long haul, COFO Pro fits the bill.

First Steps After You Buy

Before installing, measure your desktop's thickness against the clamp's supported range — this helps you avoid the frustration of the arm not fitting once it arrives. Before mounting your monitor, move the bare arm up, down, left, and right to confirm the range of motion matches what you expect, then connect the cables — this cuts down on having to redo things later. If you notice the arm sagging slightly after a few weeks of use, re-tightening the adjustment screw at the base often fixes it.
